Proposal · Material Tracking System · Nordic Bio Cosmetic Production Oy

Every pallet has an address.

Paikka is a warehouse pallet and location tracking system. Two QR codes and one phone camera keep the map on the screen identical to the pallets on the floor — and when reality and the screen disagree, Paikka is designed to notice first and heal itself.

S·01

Executive summary

You know what you own. You do not always know where it is. Paikka closes that gap with the cheapest hardware in the building — the camera already in every operator’s pocket — and a movement protocol that refuses to record a lie.

A cosmetics plant runs on pallets of caps, jars, bottles, cartons, labels and bulk raw material. Most of them look alike. A pallet of 60 mm white caps is indistinguishable from the pallet of 60 mm white caps next to it, except for the batch number printed somewhere on the shrink wrap. When production calls for material, somebody walks the aisles. When a pallet is put back “somewhere near where it came from”, nobody writes it down. Over months, the warehouse becomes a place you search rather than a place you retrieve from.

Paikka replaces searching with retrieval. Every pallet carries its own unique licence code. Every place a pallet can stand — every rack slot, every floor block, every staging lane, the quarantine cage, the dock — carries its own location code. A movement is not a memory or a note; it is a transaction made of scans, and the system will not close it until the scans agree with the physical world.

What we are proposing, in seven points

  • A unique code per pallet, not per product. This is the single most important correction to the original brief, and section 06 explains it in plain terms: a code that identifies “60 mm white cap” cannot tell two pallets of 60 mm white caps apart, so it cannot track location. The label still shows the product prominently for humans — but the code underneath is unique to that pallet, and carries its batch and quantity with it.
  • A code per location, printed on rack beams and floor markers, following a naming scheme a new employee can read without training (section 07).
  • A live graphical map of the warehouse on any screen: floor plan, rack elevations, colour-coded by occupancy and material status, click a slot to see exactly which pallet, which batch, since when, put there by whom (section 08).
  • A two-scan movement protocol — scan location, scan pallet, both ends of the journey — where a pallet is never “nowhere”: between pick and put it is held in the operator’s own custody, which is itself an address (section 09).
  • Physical proof of vacancy. A second sticker at the back of each slot, hidden by any pallet standing in it, means “this slot is empty” can be proved with the camera rather than merely ticked on a screen. This is our answer to the double confirmation you asked for, and we believe it is genuinely unusual (section 10).
  • Self-healing inventory. Every scan corrects the record it touches. A pallet found where the system did not expect it is adopted into that location on the spot, with an exception logged — not silently overwritten and not blocked (section 12).
  • An exception catalogue of 48 named failure modes, each with how the system detects it, what it does, and who resolves it (section 13). This is the part most warehouse projects discover after go-live. We would rather design it first.

S·02

The idea in one minute

If you read nothing else, read this page. Everything after it is detail.

Think of a hotel. Guests have names and passports; rooms have numbers on the doors. The hotel does not track “a Finnish couple” — it tracks which guest is in which room, from when, and it never lets two parties into room 214 at the same time without someone noticing at the front desk.

Paikka does the same for pallets. The pallet is the guest, the rack slot is the room, and the scan is checking in. The record joining the two — this pallet is in that slot, since this moment, put there by this person — is called a binding. The whole system exists to create, move and end bindings honestly.

A move is therefore just two things happening in order: a binding ends (you lifted the pallet), and a new binding begins (you set it down). Section 09 turns that into a protocol that survives interruptions, wrong scans, dead batteries and the pallet that was already in the slot you wanted.

S·03

Why the current way breaks

Nothing here is a criticism of the people doing the work. Every failure below is a failure of the method, and each one is the direct reason for a specific design decision later in this document.

Warehouses without a location system are not chaotic — they are held together by a handful of experienced people who remember where things are. That works, and it works well, right up until the moment it does not: a holiday, a night shift, a new hire, a rush order, a sudden audit.

S·04

What existing systems teach us

This wheel has been invented. It is worth knowing which parts of it to keep, which parts are enterprise overhead you do not need, and where the standard approaches leave a real gap that Paikka fills.

The licence plate idea is forty years old and still correct

Large warehouses — including Amazon’s — identify a pallet or container with what they call a Licence Plate Number (LPN). The principle: the container gets one code, the code links to everything inside it, and the whole container then moves through the building as a single transaction rather than as forty-eight separate case movements. Industry guidance is consistent on the operational rule that matters most for you: always scan the licence plate into a location — dock door, receiving, staging, problem area, rack slot — because the location scan is what turns a code into visibility.

Paikka adopts this wholesale. Our “pallet code” is an LPN. Our contribution is not the concept; it is the rigour of the protocol around the second scan, and the exception handling.

There is an international standard for the code itself

GS1 publishes the SSCC (Serial Shipping Container Code), an 18-digit identifier for a logistics unit, normally carried in a GS1-128 barcode on the pallet label and increasingly readable as a 2D code. It is what your customers and logistics partners already expect on an outbound pallet. Note one useful fact from the current GS1 “Sunrise 2027” transition: the migration to 2D codes targets consumer units; pallets and logistics units continue with SSCC in GS1-128, so adopting SSCC now is not a decision you will have to unwind.

Recommendation

Use an internal code (short, camera-friendly, printable on any label stock) for every pallet from the moment it exists, and additionally assign a GS1 SSCC to pallets that leave the site. Internal codes cost nothing and can be minted freely; SSCC numbers come from your GS1 Finland company prefix and are worth reserving for real logistics units. Appendix A specifies both, and how one label carries them together.

Cosmetics adds requirements a generic warehouse app will not have

ISO 22716 — the cosmetics GMP standard — is explicit about warehousing in ways that shape this design: incoming raw and packaging materials must be inspected and approved before use; materials must be stored in designated areas with appropriate separation; quality control must be independent of production and is the function that approves or rejects; and inventory records must show movement, usage, expiry and FIFO evidence. Batch traceability must define checkpoints and record lot numbers, supplier, dates and QC metrics at each one.

Translated into features: status on every pallet designated zones with allowed-status rules QC release as a separate role expiry-driven picking an event log you can hand an auditor. All are in scope (§18).

S·05

The vocabulary

Eight words. If everyone in the building uses them the same way, half the confusion in a warehouse project disappears before it starts.

Material

A type of thing: “60 mm white flip-top cap”, “200 ml amber glass jar”, “organic sea buckthorn extract”. Materials have specifications, suppliers and shelf lives. They do not have locations — only pallets do.

Batch

One production or delivery lot of a material, with its own expiry and its own QC verdict. Two pallets of the same material may be in different batches and must never be mixed on one pallet.

Pallet

One physical unit standing on the floor: a material, a batch, a quantity, a status. Gets a unique code the day it is created. Dies when it is emptied or shipped — and its code is never reused.

Location

Any place a pallet may legitimately stand: a rack slot, a marked floor square, a staging lane, the quarantine cage, a dock, a production point-of-use. It is a record with a code, a capacity and rules — your “placeholder”.

Binding

The live statement “pallet X is in location Y, since time T, placed by person P”. Created by a put-away, ended by a pick. Never edited — corrections create new bindings and leave the old one visible in history.

Custody

The address a pallet has while it is on somebody’s forks. Custody is a location like any other — it just moves around the building with the operator. This is why a pallet is never “nowhere”.

Movement

One complete journey: pick from A, put to B. It is a single transaction with two halves. An unfinished movement is visible to everybody as an open item, not as silence.

Exception

Any moment where the physical world and the record disagree, or where a rule blocks an action. Exceptions are first-class objects with an owner, a state and a clock — not error messages that vanish.

Naming note

Paikka is Finnish for “place, spot, slot”. The whole system is one question — missä se on?, where is it — so the name is the question.

S·06

Identity: codes & labels

Your brief proposed one QR code per type of material. We need to change that, and this is the most consequential paragraph in the document — so here it is in plain language, with the reasoning shown.

Why the code must be per pallet, not per product

Suppose all nine pallets of 60 mm white caps carry the same QR code, CAP-60-WHT. An operator scans that code in slot A-03-2-B. The system now knows: a pallet of white caps is in A-03-2-B. Tomorrow another operator scans the same code in C-11-1-A. Is that the same pallet, moved? A different one? Did the first one leave? The system cannot tell — and neither can you. With a shared code, the map can show that white caps exist somewhere, which you already knew. It cannot show where this pallet is, which is the entire point.

Worse for a cosmetics manufacturer: a shared code cannot carry a batch. Two batches of the same cap, one released and one still in quarantine, become indistinguishable to the scanner — and that is a GMP failure, not just an inconvenience.

Design rule 01

One code, one pallet, forever. The code identifies the physical unit, not the product. The product, batch, quantity and expiry are printed large on the same label for humans, and travel with the code in the database — so you still get everything you wanted from a “code per material”, plus the ability to actually track location. Codes are never reused, even years after the pallet is gone.

The code format

Short enough to type when a label is torn, structured enough to read aloud over radio, and unmistakably not a phone number.

PALLET      NBC-P-000241-7
            │   │ │      └─ check character (Damm algorithm, catches
            │   │ └───────── single-digit and transposition typos)
            │   └─────────── sequence, minted by the server only
            └─────────────── site prefix + object kind (P = pallet)

LOCATION    A-03-2-B
            │ │  │ └─ slot within the level (A = left, B = right)
            │ │  └─── level, counted from the floor upward
            │ └────── bay, numbered along the aisle
            └──────── rack row

VACANCY     A-03-2-B•V     the seal at the back of that slot (§10)

The QR code on each label encodes a web address rather than a bare string:

https://paikka.nordicbio.fi/p/NBC-P-000241-7      ← pallet
https://paikka.nordicbio.fi/l/A-03-2-B            ← location

This costs nothing and buys three things. A visitor, a driver or an auditor who scans the label with the plain camera app gets a readable page instead of gibberish. The Paikka app recognises its own domain and handles the scan natively, offline, without a network round trip. And the pattern matches GS1 Digital Link, so the same label can later carry a GS1-resolvable identity for outbound logistics without a re-labelling exercise. Appendix A gives the full payload grammar, including the offline-parseable compact form.

The map on the screen

The picture in figure 04 is not a drawing made for this document. It is the product — rendered live from the location register, repainted the moment a scan lands.

Four views, one model

Floor plan

The whole warehouse from above, zones and rack rows in true relative position. Colour shows state, click any bay to open it. This is the screen for the wall monitor in the warehouse office — glance at it and you know how full you are and where the problems are.

Rack elevation

One bay, front-on, exactly as the operator sees it (figure 05). Levels and slots in physical arrangement, so the screen and the steel agree. Tap a slot for the pallet, the batch, the age and the last four movements.

Find it

Type or scan a material, batch or order. The map dims everything else and lights up the matching pallets, sorted by expiry. Big “take this one” marker on the recommended pallet, with the walking route implied by the highlight.

Attention list

Not a map at all — the queue of things that need a human: contested slots, overdue custody, stale locations, quarantine expiring, pallets whose batch is nearing its date. Sorted by age, owned by a person, each with one button that starts the fix.

The colour language, and why colour is never alone

Five states, five colours, used identically everywhere: free occupied & released quarantine or expiring reserved or in transit contested or blocked. Roughly one man in twelve has some form of colour vision deficiency, and a warehouse is exactly the population where that matters, so every state also carries a distinct border treatment (solid, dashed, heavy) and a text label at any zoom level where the slot is large enough to hold one. Nothing in Paikka is communicated by hue alone.

The mobile app

Design constraints that shaped these screens: gloved hands (44 px minimum targets, no small toggles), a phone held one-handed while the other hand is on a pallet truck, bright dock daylight and dim aisle lighting (high-contrast, torch auto-on below a light threshold), and noise (no reliance on sound — confirmations vibrate and flash the full screen green or red).

S·09

The movement protocol

This is the core of the system: the small set of rules that decide whether a move is allowed to be recorded, and what happens when reality refuses to cooperate.

The shape of a movement

Every movement is one transaction with two halves, and each half is location first, pallet second. Always that order, everywhere in the app, so it becomes muscle memory: where am I, what am I touching.

HalfScan 1Scan 2What the system does
PickA-03-2-BNBC-P-000241-7 Verifies the pallet is the one the record expects there. Ends the binding. Opens custody in the operator’s name. The slot turns free immediately — it really is free, the pallet is on the forks.
PutC-11-1-ANBC-P-000241-7 Checks the destination is free, permitted for this material and status, and within weight and size limits. Closes custody. Opens the new binding. Publishes to every open map within a second.

Carrying more than one pallet

A forklift with double forks, or an operator moving a stack of two, holds custody of a list, not a single pallet. The app shows the list, the put step asks which pallet is going into this slot, and custody closes when the list is empty. Rule R6 counts the list, not the trip.

Putting it back where it came from

This is the case your brief highlights, and in Paikka it needs no special handling at all: the slot was released the moment the pallet was picked, so returning to it is an ordinary put into a free slot. If someone else took that slot in the meantime — entirely legitimate, and the map showed it as free — the operator gets the conflict screen and one of the three exits in the next section. That is the whole point of releasing on pick rather than on put.

S·10

Proving a slot is empty

You asked for a double confirmation before a pallet is committed to a location that may still hold something. Here it is, and the second confirmation is physical — something the building itself can testify to, not a checkbox an operator can tap through.

The vacancy seal

At the back of every rack slot, on the rear beam or the back stop, we apply a second small label: the slot’s code with a •V suffix, in warning yellow, dashed border, unmistakably not a location label. A pallet standing in that slot covers it. The geometry does the work: if your camera can read the vacancy seal, there is no pallet in front of it.

Design rule 02

Vacancy is proved, not asserted. Whenever the system believes a slot is occupied and the operator believes it is empty, the disagreement is settled by scanning the seal — not by an “are you sure?” dialog. A tap proves willingness; a seal scan proves a fact about the physical world.

Two confirmations therefore stand behind every contested put-away, and they are independent of each other:

  • Physical confirmation — the seal scan (or, for floor blocks and lanes where a seal cannot be covered, a counted confirmation: the operator scans every pallet physically present in the block, and the count must reconcile).
  • Record confirmation — the system resolves what happened to the pallet it thought was there. It is marked missing, a search task is raised, and it is not quietly forgotten. A slot cannot be re-used while its previous occupant is unaccounted for without leaving a trail.

Honest limitations, stated up front: on levels 3 and above, a seal at the back of a 1.2 m deep slot may be out of camera range from the aisle floor. Our answer is not to pretend otherwise — it is that seals are mandatory on levels 1–2 (where the great majority of conflicts happen, because that is where hand pallet trucks work), optional above, and every slot without a readable seal falls back to the dispute path, which is a first-class outcome rather than a failure. During the phase 1 pilot we measure seal read rates per level and decide whether long-range imagers on the forklifts are worth their cost.

What happens when you arrive and the slot is not what you expected

The mirror case: the system says free, but something is standing there

Equally common, and handled by the same machinery. The operator scans the slot, the app says free, the operator sees a pallet. One tap on “something is already here”, scan it, and adoption runs: the found pallet is re-bound to this slot, its previous location is released, an exception is logged against whoever last moved it — not as blame, but so that the pattern is visible if it repeats — and the incoming pallet gets a fresh suggestion. Section 12 explains why this behaviour, applied thousands of times, makes the database converge on reality instead of drifting away from it.

S·11

Three scenes on the floor

Specifications describe what a system does. Stories tell you whether people will actually use it. Names are illustrative.

Tuesday 07:40 · Goods in · Marko, warehouse operator

A delivery arrives

Twelve pallets of 200 ml amber jars come off the truck. Marko opens Paikka, taps Receive, and scans the delivery note. The purchase order is already there, so the app knows what is expected: material, quantity, supplier, batch.

For each pallet he confirms the quantity, and the label printer at the goods-in desk produces two labels. He sticks one on the long side and one on the short side, and scans one back to prove the print is readable. The status band prints yellow: QUARANTINE, because nothing is released until QC says so.

He does not choose where the pallets go. Paikka does, and it explains itself: “Q-CAGE has 4 free spaces. Put 4 here, 8 in IN-LANE-2 until QC releases.”

Two scans per pallet as he places them. Eleven minutes for the whole truck. When Liisa in QC releases the batch that afternoon, twelve status bands turn green on the map at once, and the eight pallets in the lane appear on the put-away list for whoever is free.

Tuesday 11:15 · Aisle A · Elina, production support

Half a pallet comes back — and its slot is taken

Line 2 needed 4 000 caps. Elina took a full pallet to POU-LINE-2 at nine, and now 10 400 caps are going back to the racks. She scans POU-LINE-2, scans the pallet, and the app confirms: quantity 10 400, custody hers, clock running.

Paikka suggests A-03-2-B — the slot she took it from, still free, closest to where she is standing. She drives over. There is a pallet of cartons in it.

The app does not argue with her. It asks what she sees, she taps “another pallet is here”, and scans the cartons: NBC-P-000362-4. Two seconds later: “Thanks — cartons re-registered to A-03-2-B. Your slot is A-03-3-B, one level up.”

She puts her pallet up one level, scans the slot, scans the pallet, done. Elapsed time from surprise to resolution: under a minute. Meanwhile, three things happened without her: the carton pallet’s old slot in row D was released, an exception was logged against the movement that never got recorded this morning, and the supervisor’s attention list gained one line, low priority, already resolved.

Saturday 23:05 · Row E, the far corner · Jussi, night shift

The Wi-Fi is dead and the shift still has to run

Row E sits behind a steel mezzanine and the access point there has been unreliable for a year. The app shows a thin yellow bar: offline — 3 moves queued. Jussi keeps working. Scans still validate: the codes carry their own check digits, the location register was cached on his phone at login, and the rules about which materials may go where are cached with it.

What he cannot get offline is a guarantee that a slot he thinks is free is still free — another operator might have taken it two minutes ago. So the app is honest about it: slots it cannot verify are shown hatched, with “last known free 21:40”. He picks one, puts the pallet in, and carries on.

At 23:20 he walks past the office, the phone reconnects, and eleven moves upload in under a second. Ten are accepted. One is not: somebody had taken E-06-1-A at 22:55. Paikka does not silently fix this and it does not lose the work — it raises one exception, tells Jussi exactly which pallet and which slot, and puts it at the top of his screen: “Please check E-06-1-A. Two pallets may be recorded there.”

He walks back, scans what is actually in the slot, and the record is true again. Total cost of a two-hour network outage: one walk down the aisle.

S·12

Self-healing inventory

Every warehouse database drifts from reality. The interesting question is not how to prevent drift — you cannot — but whether the system pulls itself back toward the truth or away from it.

Confidence, not certainty

Every binding carries a confidence value that decays with time since the last physical scan. A slot scanned an hour ago is near-certain. One last touched in November is a hypothesis. The map renders low-confidence slots with a hatched fill, so a supervisor can see at a glance which parts of the warehouse are well known and which are folklore.

Confidence is not decoration — it drives work. The cycle-count generator picks the lowest-confidence, highest-value, fastest-moving slots each week and issues short count tasks: fifteen slots, five minutes, done between other jobs. There is no annual stock-take weekend, because the counting never stops and never piles up.

The three healing mechanisms

Adoption

Whenever a human physically observes a pallet somewhere the record did not expect, the record yields to the observation, immediately, without a supervisor in the loop. The exception is logged, the pattern is watched, but the work is never blocked. Physical truth outranks stored truth — always.

Sweeps

Blind counts, generated from confidence decay and never showing the operator what the system expects — because a count that shows you the answer is a count that confirms the answer. Discrepancies over a threshold trigger an independent recount by a second person before any adjustment is posted.

Search tasks

A pallet reported missing does not disappear from the system — it moves to a missing state with an open search task, stays visible in stock reports as unavailable, and is automatically closed the moment anybody, anywhere, scans it. Most missing pallets find themselves within a day.

Design rule 03

Every scan makes the record truer. A scan is never merely a lookup. If what the scanner sees differs from what the database holds, the database is corrected in that moment — and the difference is recorded as evidence, not discarded. Thousands of small corrections a week beat one heroic stock-take a year.

Where healing stops and humans start

Three things are never healed automatically, because getting them wrong has consequences beyond a misplaced pallet: a change of quality status (only QC may release, hold or reject), a change of quantity beyond a small tolerance (needs a counted adjustment with a reason code and, above a threshold, a second approver), and anything touching a batch identity. Location is a logistics fact and heals itself. Status, quantity and batch are quality facts and belong to people.

S·14

Working offline

Warehouse Wi-Fi is unreliable in exactly the places you need it most: behind steel racking, in the corners, near the dock doors. An app that stops working there will be worked around, and a system that gets worked around is worse than no system.

Paikka is offline-first by default rather than offline-tolerant as an afterthought. The phone holds its own copy of what it needs and its own ledger of what it has done, and the network is treated as a bonus rather than a precondition.

What the operator sees, and what they are promised

Works offlineDegrades honestly offline
Scanning and decoding · check-digit validation · pallet and location lookup from cache · recording picks and puts · capacity, status and material rules · quantity entry · photos · exception reporting Slot freshness (shown hatched with the time it was last confirmed) · slot reservations (cannot be guaranteed, so the app says so) · label printing · live map for other people · task assignment

The promise made to the operator is narrow and always kept: your scans are never lost. The promise deliberately not made is “the slot you are looking at is definitely free”, because offline that cannot be true. Every warehouse app that pretends otherwise trains its users to distrust it.

Queue hygiene: the app syncs opportunistically on any connectivity, warns at five queued moves or ten minutes of queue age, warns loudly at thirty minutes, and shows a supervisor which devices are carrying unsynced work. Access-point coverage in the known dead spots is part of phase 0 — software resilience is a safety net, not an excuse for bad Wi-Fi.

S·15

Data model & audit trail

Small model, strict rules. Eleven tables carry the whole system, and one of them — the event log — is the only one that is truly authoritative.

Why append-only matters here specifically

Pharmaceutical and cosmetics quality auditors assess records against the data-integrity principles known as ALCOA+: attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate, plus complete, consistent, enduring and available. An append-only event log with a named actor and a server timestamp satisfies those properties by construction rather than by policy. Each event also stores the hash of its predecessor, so any tampering with history is detectable — a cheap property to add on day one and impossible to retrofit convincingly.

Corrections are therefore never edits. A wrong quantity is fixed by a correcting event that references the original, and both are visible forever. The current state is what you get by replaying the log; the history is what you get by reading it.

S·16

Architecture & technology

Deliberately boring technology. Everything here is something a Finnish developer can be hired to maintain in five years without specialist training, which matters more for a system like this than any performance benchmark.

Scanning technology, honestly assessed

Modern phone browsers decode a QR code from a printed label in well under a second at half a metre. Two caveats you should hear before committing: reading a code at four metres up a rack from an aisle floor is a job for a dedicated long-range imager, not a phone camera; and phone cameras struggle with heavy glare on shrink-wrapped labels, which is why the label spec calls for matte laminate. The build uses the browser’s native barcode detection where available with a WebAssembly decoder as fallback, so the same app runs on a €250 phone, a €900 rugged terminal and the office laptop.

The regulation makes the responsible person accountable for a product information file including manufacturing method and traceability of ingredients, and for reacting to safety issues once a product is on the market. In practice, the question that hurts is the recall question: which finished batches contain material from supplier lot X? GDPR and Finnish employment law — read this before approving the project

Requires a decision before build

Scan records are personal data. They show, minute by minute, where a named employee was and what they did. That makes this system subject to GDPR and to Finnish rules on workplace privacy and co-operation — introducing a system capable of monitoring employees is a matter for co-operation negotiations with staff before it goes live, not after. Budget time for it in phase 0, and confirm the specifics with your own employment counsel.

Our design position, which we recommend you adopt and state publicly to staff:

  • Purpose limitation, written down. Movement data exists for material traceability and for finding pallets. It is not used for individual productivity ranking, and the system deliberately ships no “scans per operator per hour” leaderboard.
  • Aggregate by default. Supervisor dashboards show team and zone level figures. Individual attribution is available where GMP requires it — on a specific event, in a specific investigation — not as a browsable report.
  • Retention split by purpose. Quality-relevant events are kept for the period your product documentation requires. Convenience data (device diagnostics, sync logs, Wi-Fi zone hints) is discarded within 90 days.
  • Transparency. Every operator can see their own history in the app. Nothing is recorded about them that they cannot read.
  • EU hosting with a data processing agreement, and no third-country transfers.

This is not only a legal position. A tracking system that staff experience as surveillance gets defeated — labels get covered, scans get batched at the end of a shift, the data rots. The privacy design and the data-quality design are the same design.

S·19

Hardware, labels & the physical rollout

Software is the cheap half of this project. The expensive half is a person on a ladder with a label gun, and it is the half that decides whether the system works.

Phase 0: the survey

Before a line of the location register is written, the warehouse gets measured: every rack row, bay, level and slot; every floor block outline; the cage; the docks; the points of use. The output is a spreadsheet of locations with their codes, dimensions, weight limits and coordinates, and it becomes the location register directly. This is also when the dead spots in the Wi-Fi get mapped, because knowing where the network fails is a design input, not an operational surprise.

Labelling standards that prevent later pain

  • One label per slot, on the beam directly beneath the slot it names — never one per bay (see figure 05).
  • Consistent height and side. Every label at the same position relative to its slot, so an operator’s eye and the camera both know where to look without thinking.
  • Matte laminate over the code. Gloss reflects the torch straight back into the lens; matte is the difference between a 0.4-second read and a frustrated operator.
  • Quiet zone respected — the blank margin around a QR code is part of the code. Labels are sized so the printed border never encroaches.
  • Floor block outlines painted, with the code on a floor-mounted marker post at the corner rather than on the floor itself, where forklift wheels destroy it within a month.
  • Vacancy seals last, after slot labels are verified, so a mislabelled slot never gets a matching seal.
  • Every label proof-scanned on application by the person applying it, using the app’s labelling mode, which walks the rack in order and refuses to advance until each code reads.

Why the Damm check character

It is a single decimal digit that detects every single-digit error and every transposition of adjacent digits — the two mistakes people actually make when typing a code from a torn label. Alternatives with letters would carry more information per character but are harder to read aloud over a noisy warehouse radio, and this code has to survive both channels.

Appendix B · Location naming convention

The naming scheme is the part of this project that outlives the software, so it is worth twenty minutes of argument now and none later.

RuleExampleReason
Rows are letters, running in one consistent direction across the buildingA … GA stranger can find row D without a map
Bays are two digits, numbered from the end nearest the main aisle, odd/even never mixed01 … 12Two digits sort correctly and leave room to extend
Levels count upward from the floor, starting at 11 = floor levelAdding a level on top never renumbers anything below
Slots within a level are letters, left to right when facing the rackA, B, C“Facing the rack” must be written on the wall sign at the end of every aisle
Non-rack locations use a zone prefix and a meaningful name, never a number aloneQ-CAGE, IN-LANE-1, POU-LINE-2Readable in a radio call and in an exception report at midnight
Reserved prefixes that are never physical placesCUSTODY:, VOID:, AMNESTY-Guarantees the rule “a pallet always has an address” can always be satisfied
