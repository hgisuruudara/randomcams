If you read only one page

The whole system in ten sentences

Written for anyone — no warehouse or software background needed.

Every storage place in the warehouse gets a permanent sticker with a code on it, like a house number. Every pallet gets a paper label printed by the system when the pallet is created, showing its product number in large digits.

When a worker puts a pallet away, they point their phone at the place, then at the pallet. That's it — two scans, about six seconds. The place is now marked as full, and it turns from green to red on a live map that everyone can see.

When the pallet is taken to production, they scan the same two things again and tap Take. The place turns back to green and is immediately offered to the next pallet. If the pallet comes back later — to the same place or a different one — it is just another two scans.

Behind all of this, the system writes down every single action with the time and the person's name, and never erases anything. Six months later you can ask “where was that pallet on 14 August, and who moved it?” and get an exact answer.

The result: no more walking the aisles looking for material, no more “I think it's in the back somewhere”, and a written history that stands up to an audit.

What it costs you

Two printed labels, a phone in each worker's pocket, and about ten minutes of training per person. Nothing else changes about how the warehouse physically works.

Part 1

The problem this solves

What happens today, and what it actually costs.

In a warehouse without tracking, the location of material lives in three unreliable places: the memory of whoever put it there, a whiteboard that is out of date by lunchtime, and a spreadsheet somebody updates when they remember. All three fail in the same way — they are correct until the moment someone moves something without telling anyone.

Searching

A worker walks the aisles looking for a pallet of caps. Ten to twenty minutes, several times a day, multiplied by every person on the floor.

Double buying

Material that cannot be found is assumed to be gone, so more is ordered — while the original pallet sits on a top shelf in another aisle.

Stale stock

Because nobody knows which pallet arrived first, the newest one gets used first and the oldest quietly ages at the back.

No history

When something goes wrong, there is no way to reconstruct who moved what, and when. Every question turns into a conversation instead of an answer.

None of these is dramatic on any single day. Together they are the reason warehouse staff feel busy without getting more done.

The goal of this system is narrow and deliberate: always know where a pallet is, and always know who moved it. It is not a stock accounting system, not a purchasing system, and not an ERP. Everything it does serves that one sentence.

Part 2

The four rules the system is built on

Everything else in this document follows from these. If you disagree with one of them, now is the moment to say so.

Rule one

Two codes, nothing more

A code on the place, a code on the pallet. Products themselves get no stickers.

Rule two

The code is a pointer

The label holds only an identifier. All the details live in the database, where they can change.

Rule three

Nothing is ever erased

Corrections are added as new lines. The history is written once and kept forever.

Rule four

Never block the forklift

When reality and the database disagree, reality wins. The system records the difference and moves on.

What actually happens when someone scans

The whole system is one loop, repeated hundreds of times a day. Two scans go in; a database update, a permanent log line and a colour change on everyone's screen come out.

The pallet label

Printed by the system the moment a pallet is created, on ordinary paper, and slipped into a plastic pallet pocket. The product number is the biggest thing on the label because that is the number people say out loud. The pallet number sits underneath in small type — the system needs it, humans mostly don't.

14250-001
Product numberYour existing number for that material. Typed once when the pallet is created; the name appears by itself.
Pallet counterAdded automatically. Simply “the first pallet of 14250 we ever had”. Nobody types this.

This is the compromise that makes the whole system work. Your staff read the label as “a pallet of 14250”, exactly as they think about it today. The system reads it as one specific pallet out of the many pallets of 14250 that may exist at the same time — which is what makes the question “which pallet is in A-01-3?” answerable at all.

Why not just the product number

If three pallets of 14250 all carried the same code, and one of them moved, the system could not tell which. Stock totals would stay right while the locations quietly became fiction — the exact failure the system exists to prevent. The three added digits cost nothing and remove the problem entirely.

The location placard

One permanent sticker per storage place, fixed to the rack beam at chest height so it can be scanned without bending or climbing. The address reads Aisle · Rack · Level, the same order a person would say it. This mirrors the convention used across the warehousing industry, where locations are addressed hierarchically from the widest area down to the individual position.

  • A-01-3 — aisle A, rack 01, level 3. A normal racking slot, one pallet.
  • FLOOR-02 — a marked floor square, which may hold several pallets.
  • GOODS-IN — the receiving area. Real, scannable, and visible on the map, so “arrived but not put away yet” is never invisible.
  • LINE-2 — beside production line 2. When a pallet goes to the line it is not lost, it is simply at the line.

All location labels are generated in one go: the administrator enters “Aisle A, racks 1 to 12, levels 1 to 4”, and the system produces a printable sheet of 48 labels ready to be stuck on the beams. Tagging the whole warehouse is an afternoon of work, once.

What the code physically contains

Nine characters, and nothing else. The pallet QR above contains the text 14250-001. Not the product name, not the quantity, not the date.

This matters more than it sounds. Quantities change, products get renamed, pallets get emptied. If those details were printed inside the code, the label would start lying the moment anything changed and would have to be reprinted constantly. Because the code holds only an identifier, the label stays true for the pallet's entire life while the database keeps the details current.

Part 4

A pallet's life, from label to bin

Six actions cover everything that can happen to a pallet. There is no seventh.

Creating a pallet

Material arrives. The worker opens Create pallet and types the product number — 14250. The name appears by itself from the product list, which is how the system stops two people from spelling the same jar two different ways. They add the quantity and press Create.

If the pallet carries more than one product stacked on it, Add another product adds a second line, and a third. Most pallets have one line; mixed pallets are handled without being made the normal case.

The system issues the next number in sequence, draws the QR code, and offers Print label. Ten seconds later the label is on the pallet.

The six actions

ActionWhat the worker doesWhat changes on the map

MergeScan the pallet being kept, then the one being emptiedThe emptied pallet's place turns green
CloseConfirm the pallet is finishedIts place turns green, the number is retired

Numbers are never reused

When pallet 14250-001 is finished, its number is retired permanently. The next pallet of that product is 14250-004, even if 001, 002 and 003 are all long gone. This works exactly like invoice numbers, and for the same reason: reusing a number destroys the history behind it.

So at any moment the warehouse holds a set of numbers with gaps in it — 004, 005, 007 — and that is the correct picture, not a fault. It also hands you something valuable for free:

Because numbers only ever count upwards, the lowest number is always the oldest pallet. “Take the lowest number first” gives you correct stock rotation with no extra feature, no training, and nothing to configure.

What each colour is allowed to mean

Two colours, and one rule that keeps them honest:

The colour follows the pallet, not the products on it. If some cartons are taken off a pallet that stays on the rack, the place stays red — because there is still a pallet standing there. The colour always matches what your eyes see in the aisle.

Colour is never the only signal. Each cell also carries its code, and occupied cells show the product number, so the screen still works for someone who cannot easily tell red from green, and still works when printed in black and white.

Finding things

Type a product number or name into the search box and every place holding it lights up on the map, oldest pallet first. A worker sent to fetch caps gets an answer in three seconds instead of a walk down four aisles — the single change that people notice most in the first week.

Part 7

What the system remembers

Five simple lists. A person who can read a spreadsheet can read this.

Why the log is the important one

The first four lists tell you what is true right now. The fifth tells you what has happened, and it is the reason this system can be trusted rather than merely believed.

Corrections are made by adding a line, never by rubbing one out. If a pallet turns up somewhere it should not be, the system records both the belief and the correction, with the time and the person. Nothing in the app can quietly rewrite yesterday.

That single decision is what turns a convenience tool into an audit record — and it costs nothing to implement, as long as it is decided at the beginning rather than bolted on later.

Part 8

When things go wrong

This is the part that decides whether people trust the system or quietly stop using it. Every case below has a defined answer.

A tracking system fails in one of two ways. Either it blocks people from doing their job, so they work around it and the data becomes fiction — or it accepts everything silently, so the data becomes fiction anyway. The way through is a single principle:

The system almost never stops a worker. It records what really happened, marks the difference for someone to look at, and lets the pallet keep moving. There are exactly two hard stops in the whole application.

Those two are: placing a pallet into a place that already holds one, and placing a pallet into a place marked out of service. Both are physically impossible, so blocking them prevents nonsense rather than preventing work.

The weekly ten minutes

Everything the system could not resolve by itself lands on one screen: Differences. Phantom pallets, adopted pallets, overrides, forgotten transits. A supervisor works through the list once a week — usually three or four items — and each decision is recorded with a reason.

A tracking system with no such list is not clean; it is simply hiding its disagreements. This one puts them in a single place and expects ten minutes of attention a week.

Part 9

What everyone else does, and where we differ

This design is not invented from nothing. Three things were checked before settling it.

1. The pallet identifier

Every serious warehouse system in the world puts a unique identifier on the physical handling unit rather than on the product it contains. The industry name for it is a licence plate: a unique number applied to a pallet or container so that the whole unit can be moved, stored and found as one thing, with the item numbers, quantities and lot numbers linked to it in the database rather than printed on it. Our 14250-001 is exactly that idea, dressed in your own product numbering so it reads naturally to your staff.

2. The location addressing

Warehouse labelling practice addresses storage places hierarchically — zone, aisle, bay, level, position — and puts a scannable label on every one of them, numbered consistently left to right and bottom to top. Our A-01-3 is a deliberately shortened version of the same convention: short enough to say out loud, long enough to extend when you add a second hall.

3. Cosmetics manufacturing rules

ISO 22716, the good manufacturing practice standard for cosmetics, expects raw materials to be stored in designated areas with inventory records showing movement and usage, batch-level traceability, and first-in-first-out stock rotation. This system delivers the designated areas, the movement records and — through the counting numbers — the stock rotation. It does not yet carry batch numbers or expiry dates, and that gap is deliberate and reversible; see Part 13.

Part 10

How it is put together

In plain language, for the person who has to approve it rather than write it.

There is no app to install from an app store. The workers open a web address on their phone once, add it to the home screen, and from then on it looks and behaves like an ordinary app — including working when the phone has no signal. This is a normal, well-supported way to build warehouse tools, and it removes an entire category of problems: no app store approval, no version stuck on somebody's old phone, no per-device licence.

The camera scanning uses the phone browser's own barcode reader where it exists, with a small built-in fallback where it doesn't, so the app works on both Android and iPhone without special hardware. Handheld scanners can be added later for high-volume stations; nothing in the design depends on them.

Where it runs

A single small server, either in your own office or at a Finnish or EU hosting provider for a few euros a month. All data stays in the EU. No part of the design requires a cloud service you cannot replace.

What it is written in

Ordinary, boring, well-documented tools — a standard web framework and a PostgreSQL database. Chosen so that any competent developer can pick it up in a day, which matters far more than cleverness.

Who can see what

Workers sign in with a short PIN and can scan and look things up. Administrators manage products, places and users. Everyone's actions are named in the log; nobody can edit history.

If it disappeared tomorrow

The database exports to Excel in one click, and every code is printed in plain text on every label. You would lose convenience, not information.

Part 11

Getting it into the warehouse

Eight weeks, and the largest single task is not software.

Week 1

Name the warehouse

Walk the floor and give every storage place its address. This is the foundation of everything else, it needs your people rather than a developer, and it is the step most likely to be underestimated. Decide what counts as a place: which racks are one pallet, which floor zones hold several.

Week 1–2

Print and stick the placards

The system generates the full sheet of location labels. Two people with a ladder and a roll of tape complete a normal hall in a day. Take photographs of each aisle when finished — they become the map layout.

Week 1–4

Build the core

Create, place, take, close, the map, the log, offline queueing, label printing. Nothing else. The features left out in Part 13 stay out.

Week 5

Pilot one aisle

Two volunteers, one aisle, one week, alongside the current way of working. The goal is not to prove it works; it is to find the three things nobody thought of. Expect to change wording, button size and the order of two screens.

Week 6

Fix what the pilot found

Then load the real product list and register every pallet already standing in the warehouse — the one genuinely tedious day in the project. Do it aisle by aisle, and do not start until the pilot's changes are in.

Week 7

Go live on the whole floor

Ten minutes of training per person, standing at a rack with a phone, not in a meeting room. A printed one-page card in the goods-in area. The wall screen goes up the same day — seeing the map change is what makes people believe it.

Week 8 onward

Settle into the rhythm

The supervisor clears the differences list once a week. After a month of clean running, decide whether batch numbers and expiry dates are worth adding.

The one thing that decides success

Scanning must be faster than not scanning. If finding a free place through the app is quicker than walking the aisle to look for one, people will scan without being asked. If it is slower, no amount of policy will keep the data true. Every design decision in this document was made with that sentence in mind.

What happens next
